Methodology

The score

{{ meta.score_formula }}

Two anchors, both measured on this part:

  • TSOL — the roofline: the time this workload would take if it were limited only by the arithmetic it must do and the bytes it must move. Nothing can beat it, so S = 1 is unreachable in practice.
  • Tb — the anchor: the fastest of four PyTorch formulations of the reference, measured on an idle GPU 0 under a locked clock. S = 0.5.

Where the bounds came from

TSOL is computed in cycles, which is architectural and therefore independent of the clock, then divided by the clock that datapath actually holds. Two derivations run independently and are combined with max:

FLOCK = {{ meta.f_lock_mhz }} MHz is what the part holds under a dense bf16 matrix-core load, and every matrix path does hold it — bf16 1296, fp16 1299, fp8 1314, measured under 20 s of saturating GEMM each. The fp32 vector path does not: it draws far less power and sustains 1441 MHz. Dividing every bound by one clock made 805 fp32 and fp8 bounds too large, which is why manifest v1.1 divides each by its own datapath's measured clock. Cycle counts did not change.

How a submission is timed — and one way that went wrong

Each timed iteration is bracketed by two GPU events recorded on the default stream, and the loop does not synchronize between iterations, so the driver's queue stays full and launch latency does not leak into the number.

Until 2026-08-10 that bracket could not see work a submission put on a stream of its own. With the queue deliberately kept full, a freshly created side stream is empty, so work launched there ran immediately — against earlier iterations — and its duration never landed inside the bracket that paid for it. Measured on this part: a 12.06 ms GEMM reported as 0.0069 ms.

The timed region now fences the submission's own streams from the default stream before the start event and joins them back before the end event, which is what the wait_stream idiom does by hand. A submission that creates no stream takes no new path, so no single-stream measurement moved.

Two of the published scores were affected and were re-measured on GPU 0. L1__054 went 0.14395 → 0.18948 ms and L1__055 0.05871 → 0.07320 ms; three more kernels that create a stream turned out not to have been affected and are unchanged, which is recorded rather than assumed. Nothing else in the run was re-timed, because re-timing 220 problems to correct five would move 215 numbers that had nothing wrong with them.

Worth being plain about what this changes in how the rest of this page reads. L1__054 was on the known-wrong-bounds list below and is not any more — and its bound was never wrong. It was scoring a mean of 1.07, above the roofline, because its time was 32% short. Meanwhile L1__055 was undercounted by 25% and appeared on no list at all, because a kernel scoring 0.57 can be inflated by 10% without crossing 1. “A kernel beat its TSOL” is one symptom with several causes, and it only fires when the error is large enough to break that single invariant.

What this board does not cover

One part, one clock. Every score here was measured on {{ meta.part or meta.device }} at FLOCK {{ meta.f_lock_mhz }} MHz. TSOL in milliseconds, Tb and FLOCK all differ by part, so a score measured on another part cannot be ranked against these — scripts/score_solutions.py refuses that rather than rescaling.

The other part this port targets, MI355X (the liquid-cooled 1400 W CDNA4 part), is in the part switch with nothing behind it. Selecting it lands on an empty state, and that is the accurate page: no clock has been measured on it, so it has no FLOCK, and without FLOCK there is no TSOL in milliseconds, no Tb and no S. The port itself needs no work for it — every number does. The runbook is docs/TODO-MI355X.md, and when it has been executed that part gets its own database, not extra rows on this one.

This paragraph used to print “FLOCK 1640” for MI355X. No such measurement exists: that part's clock preset requests 1650 and declares no achieved_gpu_clk_mhz, and --setperfdeterminism X does not give you X — on MI350X it yields about 0.83·X, and whether that ratio holds at 1400 W has never been asked. The same 1640 is recorded in STATE.md as the stamp on 143 artifacts that were really running near 1860 MHz. A requested clock is not an achieved one, so no clock is quoted here for a part nobody has measured.

Problems whose bound is known to be wrong

A submitted kernel was measured faster than TSOL on these problems. Nothing beats the roofline, so this is a defect in the bound rather than a result. Those workloads are stored with no score so they cannot lift any ranking, and are still shown on the problem page.

There were thirteen of these under manifest v1 and they were carried as thirteen defects. They were not thirteen defects, and they were not even all bound defects. 13 → 3, in three steps:

  • v1.1 corrected the paged-cache traffic term (D18) and the single FLOCK divisor (D35). 13 → 6.
  • The timer, not a bound: L1__054's TSOL was right and its measured time was 32% short, because the harness did not see work the kernel put on its own stream (above). 6 → 5.
  • v1.2 corrected SOLAR's pricing of a grouped convolution: it read groups only from nn.Module arguments, and every convolution here is functional, so a depthwise convolution was costed as though every input channel fed every output channel — over by exactly the group count, 768× on L1__006's arithmetic term. 5 → 3.
  • Then coverage put two back. A second model finishing all 220 problems exposed L1__018 and L1__042, which nothing had reached before. That is the pattern rather than an accident: a bound is only shown wrong by a kernel that beats it, so this count tracks how hard anything has tried and not how many wrong bounds there are. The figure above is read from the database, so it moves on its own.

D18, the paged‑cache traffic defect: the bound priced a KV cache at its full allocation while the kernel gathers only the pages kv_indices names. For one problem the bound was the same 185,274 cycles for every workload, which is exactly the time to stream the whole 1.140 GB allocation once. D35, the clock: TSOL was divided by a single FLOCK that only the matrix‑core paths hold (see above). Six of the thirteen were that alone.

D35 is the one worth understanding as a reader, because the list below was never the set of inflated bounds. Three kernels that have never been beaten by anything were measured holding the same high clock as the ones that did — one of them higher than any violation on this board. The list is the subset where a kernel got good enough to expose a bound, not the subset of bounds that were wrong.

The grouped-convolution correction is the proof of that. Six problems had it, and only some had ever appeared on this list — the others were not the ones whose bounds were right, they were the ones whose bounds were too wrong for anything to get near. All six are fixed in v1.2. Which correction applies to which problem is recorded in STATE.md D18, D35, D36, D37, D38 and D42, not inferred here.

Two earlier statements here were wrong and are corrected rather than removed. This paragraph said seven problems, from an AST scan that searched the whole reference file instead of the traced graph; it is six. And it named L2__036 as the witness, which it is not: that problem's bound errs in the opposite direction — its reference runs a 128-iteration Python loop the traced graph barely contains, so its arithmetic term is near zero and its bound is far too small. It belongs in the section below, not this one.

Five open, and they are two causes

Diagnosed against manifest v1.2 in artifacts/11/bad-bounds-v12.json. Each mechanism was checked by hand-computing the bound from the problem's own declared shapes; a mechanism is only recorded when that computation lands on the manifest number, and all of them do.

  • Three of the five are one defect, and it is D18 again. The declared-traffic tier prices every declared input at its full allocation, regardless of what the kernel reads. L1__018: the whole 262,144-slot k+v cache, read and written, 2.15 GB, for seq_len slots touched. L1__057: the 157,184-row embedding table, 1.29 GB, for a gather of B×S rows. L1__042: expert_mask and topk_idx, which run() never reads at all — exactly 65/32 = 2.03125× on all sixteen workloads. v1.1 fixed this for the two paged problems rather than at the tier, so it is still live: 328 workloads across 38 problems are scored against a bound where the traffic tier won, p50 1.50× and max 128.9×. Most produce no violation only because nothing has got close enough.
  • One is SOLAR counting arithmetic the reference discards. L2__045 prices the Q-Former and projector over every window, and the reference reads only the first few: 21.5× of the counted MACs never reach the output. A second, independent error partly masks it — those fp32 einsums are priced at the bf16 MAC rate, exactly 1/16 — and that one is in the safe direction, so nothing reported it. It hid the first for long enough that only a kernel doing both could expose either.
  • One is not a modelling error. L2__073's worst violation is 0.992, inside the band D35 predicts for a compute-bound fp32 workload clocking above the 1300 MHz divisor.

No bound is corrected by that diagnosis. A hand check that reproduces a manifest number proves what the number is, not what it should be. Every correction so far has been a re-derivation with an exact ratio behind it, and none has been an adjustment made until a violation went away.

The list above is every bound a kernel was measured faster than. That is the only automatic check there is on a bound, and it can only catch a TSOL that is too large. A TSOL that is too small breaks no rule at all: it is a perfectly valid lower bound, just a weak one, so nothing reports it.

It still costs the reader something. Since S = 1 / (1 + (T_k − T_SOL) / (T_b − T_SOL)), as TSOL falls toward zero the score becomes T_b / (T_b + T_k) — a comparison against the PyTorch anchor with no roofline left in it. Headroom below is Tb / TSOL: how much range the score actually has to work with.

Two of these are self-inflicted and worth saying so. The paged FlashInfer problems are near the top because manifest v1.1 corrected them: pricing a KV cache at the pages it gathers rather than at its whole allocation was right, and it took FlashInfer-Bench__018 from 185,274 cycles to 8. Those went from wrong-and-tight to right-and-empty, and the pipeline could not notice because its one check looks the other way.

Every workload now carries the band as a word. On each problem page the headroom figure is followed by vacuous, loose or narrow where it applies — nothing at all in the ordinary case, because a marker on every row is a marker on none. It changes no score. It says what a score is worth, which is the thing the four decimal places cannot say for themselves.

Reading the leaderboard honestly

  • Benchmark score sums per-workload scores over every scoreable workload and counts anything not passed as zero. It is the headline because it cannot be improved by attempting fewer problems.
  • An asterisk on the benchmark score means the submission was not run on all {{ meta.scoreable_workloads }} scoreable workloads. The untested ones are still in the denominator, counting as zero exactly like failures, so a starred score is a floor rather than a measurement. Every row on the board is starred today.
  • Mean (attempted) divides by the workloads a submission attempted, and an attempt that failed counts as zero — it does not leave the average. Dividing by passes instead would put torch.compile (0.4907) above eager PyTorch (0.4548) purely because the 585 workloads it raises on would vanish from the denominator. It is never shown without coverage: on its own, any mean rewards attempting less.
  • Reference variants sit at 0.5 by construction. They define Tb; they are on the board to calibrate the scale, not to compete.
  • Flagged counts workloads the harness's anti-reward-hack check rejected — status == REWARD_HACK, an output accepted without the work being done.
